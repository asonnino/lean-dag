Uncertified DAG consensus, machine-checked

Protocols, properties and mechanisms, formalised in Lean 4
  • This talk presents a family of DAG-based consensus protocols in which blocks carry no certificates, and a formalisation of them in the proof assistant Lean 4 over its mathematics library Mathlib.
  • Every theorem cited in the deck is checked by the Lean kernel.
  • Whether the definitions state what the published papers mean has been checked only by human and LLM review, so the definitions should be read critically.
  • The background figure is a concrete DAG of 4 validators over rounds 0 to 5, taken from one of the witnesses used in the proofs.

The problem: replicated agreement

  • n validators each keep a copy of one ordered log (a ledger).
  • Clients submit transactions to any validator.
  • Safety: no two correct validators output conflicting logs.
  • Liveness: every transaction from a correct client is eventually in every correct validator’s log.
  • Some validators may be faulty. The protocol must tolerate them without identifying them.
  • The problem is replicated agreement: n validators each maintain a copy of one ordered log of transactions.
  • In the diagram each row is a validator and each column a log position; v0, v1 and v2 hold tx₁, tx₂, tx₃, and the faulty v3 outputs tx₉ at position 2.
  • Safety requires that no two correct validators output conflicting logs; the output of a faulty validator such as v3 is unconstrained.
  • Liveness requires that every transaction submitted by a correct client eventually appears in the log of every correct validator.
  • This slide is background from the literature and is not itself a theorem; in the development the log is the sequence of committed leaders with their histories, and two validators' logs are proved in Lean to agree on every prefix of slots both have decided.
  • The protocol must tolerate faulty validators without knowing which they are; the next slide states how many.

Faults and quorums

  • A Byzantine validator may deviate arbitrarily from the protocol: send false messages, send none, or send different messages to different validators (equivocate).
  • A crashed validator stops and never resumes.
  • With n ≥ 3f + 1 validators, at most f Byzantine, a quorum is q = n − f validators.
  • Two quorums share at least n − 2f ≥ f + 1 validators, so at least one correct validator.
  • A correct validator does not equivocate, so two quorums cannot support two conflicting statements.
  • With n ≥ 3f + 1 validators and quorums of q = n − f, any two quorums share at least one correct validator.
  • A Byzantine validator may deviate arbitrarily, including equivocation, that is, sending different messages to different validators; a crashed validator stops permanently.
  • In the model f bounds the Byzantine validators only, and a crashed validator counts as correct; the liveness theorems name separately the validators that keep producing blocks.
  • The diagram has n = 4, f = 1: the quorums Q₁ = {v0, v1, v2} and Q₂ = {v1, v2, v3} share the shaded set {v1, v2}, of which v1 is Byzantine and v2 is correct.
  • In general |Q₁ ∩ Q₂| ≥ 2(n − f) − n = n − 2f ≥ f + 1, and at most f of those are Byzantine; this intersection property is proved in Lean and the safety arguments rest on it.
  • Since a correct validator does not equivocate, the shared correct validator prevents two quorums from supporting two conflicting statements.

What the network is assumed to do

  • Messages can be delayed arbitrarily, up to an unknown time GST (global stabilisation time).
  • A reliable validator is a correct one that keeps producing blocks. Liveness assumes a quorum of them.
  • After GST, what one reliable validator holds reaches every reliable validator within Δ. This is called view convergence.
  • Safety is proved with no assumption about the network.
  • Liveness is proved from view convergence and the protocol’s own rules.
  • Safety is proved with no assumption about the network; liveness assumes partial synchrony in the form of view convergence.
  • Partial synchrony is the standard model from the literature and is taken as background here: messages may be delayed arbitrarily until an unknown global stabilisation time GST.
  • In the diagram the two horizontal lines are the timelines of reliable validators w and v, and the arrows are messages; before the dashed line GST arrival times are arbitrary, after it each arrival falls in a shaded window of length Δ.
  • A reliable validator is a correct validator that keeps producing blocks, and liveness assumes a set of reliable validators of quorum size.
  • View convergence states that, for t ≥ GST and reliable v, w, every block w holds at time t is held by v at time t + Δ.
  • The liveness hypothesis also contains rules about how validators behave; these are part of the protocol, and the network contributes only view convergence.

Blocks and the DAG

  • Validators work in rounds. In each round each validator creates one block: a round number, its creator, references to earlier blocks, and a payload of transactions.
  • A valid block at round r > 0 references only blocks of round r − 1, at most one per creator, from at least n − f creators, its own creator among them.
  • The blocks and references form a directed acyclic graph (DAG). A block’s history is every block it reaches by references. A validator’s view is the set of blocks it holds, including their histories.
  • Blocks are named by identifier (a hash), so two validators may hold different sets of blocks but agree on what each identifier means.
  • The state of the protocol is a DAG of blocks, built round by round.
  • A block holds a round, a creator, references to earlier blocks and a payload; a block at round r > 0 is valid when it references only blocks of round r − 1, at most one per creator, from at least n − f creators, and includes a block by its own creator.
  • In the figure rows are validators and columns are rounds 0 to 2, with the Byzantine v0 shaded; block 8, by v0 at round 2, references blocks 4, 5, 6 of round 1, three creators, which meets n − f = 3, and block 4 is v0's own previous block.
  • The history of a block is every block it reaches by references, and a validator's view is the set of blocks it holds, which is closed under references.
  • Blocks are named by identifiers (hashes), so two validators may hold different sets of blocks while agreeing on the content of every identifier they share.
  • Some later rules change validity: Nemo uses a majority and drops the self-reference and distinct-creator conditions, FinWhale replaces the self-reference by an exclusion condition on leaders, and Bluestreak requires a quorum only of leader blocks.

Certified and uncertified DAGs

  • In a certified DAG (Narwhal, Bullshark), each block receives a certificate, built by an extra exchange of signatures, before others may reference it.
  • In an uncertified DAG (Mysticeti and this family), no certificate is sent.
  • A block two rounds above a leader is a certificate for it when its references include q = n − f votes for it, that is, blocks that reference the leader.
  • Every direct commit rule is then a threshold on a set of blocks found in the graph.
  • In an uncertified DAG a certificate is a pattern of blocks in the graph, so no extra message exchange is needed.
  • Panel (a) describes certified DAGs such as Narwhal and Bullshark, where n − f signatures on a block are aggregated into a separate certificate message before round r + 1 may reference it; this is background and is not formalised here.
  • In panel (b) rows are validators, columns rounds 0 to 2, and v0 is Byzantine; block 0 is the leader L, the three votes 4, 5, 6 reference it, and block 7 does not.
  • A vote for L is a block of round r + 1 that references L, and a certificate is a block of round r + 2 whose references include votes for L from q = n − f distinct creators.
  • Blocks 8, 9, 10 reference all three votes and are certificates, which is checked in Lean for block 8 on this DAG; block 11 references only the votes 5 and 6, so 2 votes is below q.
  • Each direct commit rule in the family is then a threshold on a set of blocks found in the graph; the next slide says which block is the leader.

Slots and the leader schedule

  • A slot k has a round and a leader. The schedule may put several leaders in one round and a leader in every round.
  • The candidates of slot k are the blocks of its leader at its round. A correct leader has at most one candidate; a Byzantine leader may have several.
  • Each slot is decided as commit (one candidate) or skip.
  • The ledger is the committed leaders in slot order, each followed by the blocks in its history not yet output.
  • Slots fix which validator's block is considered at which round and the order in which those blocks are decided.
  • A slot k has a round and a leader; in the table the top schedule has one leader per round rotating through v0 to v3, so slot k is at round k, and the bottom schedule has two leaders per round, slots 2r and 2r + 1.
  • The candidates of slot k are the blocks of its leader at its round; a correct leader has at most one, and an equivocating Byzantine leader may have several, which is checked on a concrete DAG with two candidates.
  • Each slot is decided as commit, which selects one candidate, or as skip.
  • The ledger lists the committed leader blocks in slot order, each followed by the blocks of its history that have not yet been output; the next slide gives the relation that decides a slot.

One decision relation for every rule

A slot is decided in one of four ways:

  1. Direct commit: the rule’s commit condition holds in the validator’s view.
  2. Direct skip: the rule’s skip condition holds.
  3. Indirect commit: a later committed slot (the anchor) links a candidate.
  4. Indirect skip: the anchor links no candidate.

The anchor is the nearest eligible committed slot above; every eligible slot in between must be skipped.

  • Every rule in the family uses one decision relation with four cases.
  • A direct commit or direct skip holds when the rule's commit or skip condition holds in the validator's view.
  • Otherwise the slot is decided from an anchor, a later committed slot j: j must be the nearest eligible committed slot above k, every eligible slot strictly between them must be skipped, and slot k commits a candidate that the anchor links, with a tie-break choosing among several, or is skipped when the anchor links none.
  • The flowchart orders the questions for presentation only: the relation is defined inductively, as four derivation rules, and a slot with no derivation is undecided.
  • For every rule satisfying the laws of the next slide it is proved in Lean that two validators who decide the same slot decide the same value, whether by direct or indirect cases.

A rule is a card of thresholds

Every rule fills in the same record:

  • the wave: how many rounds above the proposal the direct rules read;
  • the commit and skip conditions;
  • the link from an anchor, possibly in several graded rungs;
  • a tie-break between linked candidates.

Agreement and the agreement of ledgers are proved once, for every rule whose card satisfies a short list of laws.

  • Each rule is an instance of one record of parameters, and agreement is proved once for every instance that satisfies a short list of laws.
  • The fields are the wave, the number of rounds above the proposal that the direct rules read; the commit and skip conditions; the link from an anchor, possibly graded into rungs that are tried in order; and a tie-break between linked candidates.

Eligibility

  • An anchor at slot j is eligible for slot k when round(k) + wave < round(j).
  • A slot one round above k (5) cannot reach a certificate for k’s leader. A slot two rounds above (10) reaches one only if its own block is a certificate.
  • Anchoring at such slots would let one validator’s direct commit become another validator’s indirect skip.
  • An anchor at slot j is eligible for slot k only when round(k) + wave < round(j).
  • In the figure there is one leader per round, and the leader blocks 0, 5, 10, 15 sit at rounds r to r + 3; the badges evaluate the test with wave 2, which fails at r + 1 and r + 2 and holds at r + 3, and these three results are checked in Lean.
  • Certificates for slot k are blocks of round r + 2, so block 5 at r + 1 cannot reach one, and block 10 at r + 2 reaches only round r + 1 blocks besides itself, so it links slot k only if it is itself a certificate.
  • If such slots could anchor, a validator could indirectly skip slot k through an anchor that links nothing while another validator directly commits it; excluding them is the purpose of the condition.
  • The condition is upward-closed, so the nearest eligible committed slot is well defined.
  • The leaders of this schedule are drawn on a witness DAG for illustration; only the eligibility tests depend on the schedule.

A skip must count blocks

  • The absence of the leader’s block ? from a view is not a sound reason to skip: a validator cannot distinguish a silent leader from a leader whose block has not yet arrived.
  • In Mysticeti a direct skip needs n − f blocks of the round above that reference no candidate; each such block blames the slot. Those blocks exist, and later blocks do not alter them.
  • A direct skip must rest on a count of blocks that exist, and the absence of a block is not such a count.
  • Figure (1) shows a view that ends at round 5; slot 2 is at round 6 with leader v1, the dashed outline marks its block, which has not arrived, and no block of round 7 blames the slot, so the count is 0, below q.
  • A validator cannot distinguish a silent leader from a leader whose block is delayed; in Mysticeti a block blames a slot when it is in the round above the slot and references no candidate, and a direct skip needs n − f blaming blocks, which, once held, remain in every larger view with the same references.
  • It is proved in Lean that on this DAG, grown only to round 5, no view directly skips slot 2.
  • Step 1: figure (2) shows the same DAG grown to round 8: the leader block 25 has the four votes 28 to 31 and the four certificates 32 to 35, so slot 2 is directly committed, which is proved in Lean; a skip based on the absence at round 5 would have contradicted this commit.
  • Step 1: other rules skip differently, Nemo never directly and FinWhale, Hydrozoan and Bluestreak with other counts, and their slides give the details.

How the results are checked

  • Every definition and theorem is written in Lean 4 and checked by its kernel.
  • A witness is a small concrete DAG, usually 4 to 9 validators, on which Lean computes the verdicts. Witnesses show that hypotheses can be met and that bounds are tight; counterexamples show that a published claim fails.
  • Every DAG in this deck is drawn from a witness. Figures that are not are marked as diagrams.
