Minnow: the rule

  • n ≥ 3f + 1; no self-parent rule.
  • Commit a leader block l when n − f processes reference it from the round above, and every earlier leader slot is resolved: one of its blocks is in l's history, or is concurrent with l and committed or skipped.
  • Skip when n − f processes of the round above have no block that references it.
  • The rule consults no later anchor.
  • This slide states Minnow's commit and skip conditions; the next two slides refute the rule's safety and its liveness.
  • In the diagram, the three outlined blocks of round r + 1 are n − f processes that reference the leader block l; the two green boxes on the left are the two ways in which an earlier leader slot can be resolved.
  • An earlier slot is resolved for l when some block of that slot is in l's history, or is concurrent with l (neither block is in the other's history) and is itself committed or skipped. The condition asks for some block of the slot, which need not be the block that is committed.
  • A skip counts processes: n − f processes of round r + 1 none of whose blocks references the leader block. If the skip counted blocks instead, one block could be committed and skipped at once; this is checked on a concrete DAG.
  • Minnow has no self-parent rule, and its commit rule reads only the round above the leader and the earlier slots; it consults no later anchor. The next slide shows what the existential over a slot's blocks permits when a leader equivocates.

Minnow: slots that are never decided

  • Under Minnow a slot can remain undecided in every view, and such a slot prevents the commit of every later leader whose history lacks it.

Black Marlin: the rule

  • n ≥ 3f + 1; one anchor per round; no certificates.
  • Commit an anchor B at round r when n − f validators reference it at round r + 1, and some anchor at r + 1 references B and is itself referenced by n − f validators at r + 2.
  • There is no skip. Anchors that are not committed are output through a descent from a later committed anchor.
  • This slide states Black Marlin's commit rule: an anchor is committed by two consecutive quorums, without certificates and without a skip.
  • In the diagram, B is the anchor of round r. The outlined group of round r + 1 is n − f blocks that reference B, among them the anchor A (arrow labelled link); the outlined group of round r + 2 is n − f blocks that reference A.
  • An anchor of round r is a block of that round by the validator that the rotation elects for it; an equivocating validator may have several. B is supported when n − f distinct validators reference it from round r + 1, linked when a supported anchor of round r + 1 references it, and committed when it is both.
  • There is no skip. From a committed anchor the descent moves to an anchor of the highest round in its history, choosing the anchor whose history has an anchor in the nearest round below it, and then the least identifier, and outputs the blocks below each anchor it visits.
  • The paper describes the rule as needing no reliable broadcast and no common coin; this is the paper's description, not a proved statement here. Black Marlin is not an instance of the properties interface of Part III. The next slide shows that its agreement fails.

Three recurring lessons

  1. Fix an order. Wherever two twins can pass a test, the choice between them must be made by an order every validator computes.
  2. Count, do not infer from absence. A skip must count blocks that exist.
  3. Use later evidence. Every rule proved safe here has an indirect rule that reads a later committed anchor. Minnow has none; Black Marlin's descent reads a later committed anchor, but its outputs disagree (slide 53).
  • Three requirements recur across the findings of the previous slide.
  • Fix an order: Odontoceti, Hybrid, FinWhale, Hydrozoan on its second rung, and Async BlueBottle choose the least of two twins that pass a test, and Mahi-Mahi's vote count chooses the least block.
  • Count, do not infer from absence: on a growing DAG checked in Lean, a slot whose leader has no block in some view is later committed, so a skip inferred from that absence would contradict the later commit. Mysticeti therefore skips a slot only on a count of blocks that are present.
  • Use later evidence: each of the eleven rules has at least one indirect rung that reads a later committed anchor. Minnow has no such rule; Black Marlin's descent reads a later committed anchor, but its outputs disagree (slide 53).
  • That the absence of such a rule causes the failures of Minnow and Black Marlin is an interpretation, not a proved statement; present it as such. Part III follows.
Part III

Properties

From rules to properties

Mechanisms such as garbage collection or crash recovery should not be proved once per rule. Instead:

  • each rule is proved to have a few properties;
  • each mechanism is proved once, using only those properties.

Eleven rules (all but Minnow and Black Marlin) have the properties.

  • Part III introduces an interface of properties, so that each mechanism is proved once for all eleven rules.
  • In the diagram, the rules on the left each prove the properties in the middle box, and the mechanisms on the right are each proved from the properties alone.
  • With this division, a new rule requires proofs of the properties only, and a new mechanism requires a proof from the properties only.
  • Minnow and Black Marlin are excluded: the previous slides refute them, and neither is given the properties.
  • Slide 2 shows the same three layers and lists the eleven rules. The next slide defines what a mechanism may read of a rule.

The carrier

A mechanism accesses a rule only through its carrier:

  • a type of universes (all blocks) and of views (what one validator holds);
  • the round, creator and references of each block;
  • the decision relation: under schedule S, view V decides slot k as v.

Laws: a view holds only universe blocks; a view holds every block that its blocks reference; references go one round down.

  • The carrier is the interface between rules and mechanisms: it is all that a mechanism may read of a rule.
  • In the diagram, the outer box is the universe, the set of all blocks; the blue box is one view, the blocks one validator holds; the dashed round-3 blocks are in the universe and outside the view. Every reference goes one round down.
  • The carrier gives the round, creator and references of each block, and the decision relation, drawn at the bottom: under a schedule S, which assigns each slot a round, a leader and a kind, view V decides slot k as a block L (commit) or as none (skip).
  • The decision relation is a relation, so a view may leave a slot undecided. Three laws hold for every rule: a view holds only universe blocks, a view holds every block its blocks reference, and each reference goes one round down.
  • The eleven rules are given carriers (slide 2). The next slides state the properties of the decision relation.

Banded: a verdict reads a finite band of rounds

Each verdict depends only on the blocks in a band of rounds, from the slot's round to some round top. Any other universe, view and schedule that match in the band — up to a shift of round and slot numbers — reach the same verdict.

  • Banded states that each verdict depends only on the blocks in a finite band of rounds above its slot.
  • Definition: for each verdict there is a round top such that any universe, view and schedule that agree with the original from the slot's round to top reach the same verdict. Agreement here means the same blocks, creators and references up to a shift of round numbers, the other view holding at least the original view's blocks in the band, and the same slot rounds, leaders and kinds up to a shift of slot numbers.

Agree, CommitsCandidate, Indirect

  • Agree: two views of one universe never decide a slot differently, skips included.
  • CommitsCandidate: a committed block is a candidate of its slot.
  • Indirect: a committed eligible anchor, with every eligible slot in between skipped, decides the slot; changing the other slots' leaders does not change that verdict.
  • Three further properties: verdicts agree across views, a commit names a real candidate, and a later committed anchor decides earlier slots.
  • Agree: under one schedule and over one universe, any two views that decide a slot reach the same verdict, a skip counted as a verdict. The safety theorem uses it.
  • CommitsCandidate: a committed block is a block of the universe, at the slot's round, by the slot's leader. It does not follow from Banded, which constrains which DAGs a verdict distinguishes and says nothing about what the committed identifier denotes. Safety, inclusion and chain quality use it.
  • Step 1: in the diagram, slot j holds a committed anchor that is eligible for slot i, where eligibility depends only on the rounds of the two slots; every eligible slot between them is skipped. Indirect states that slot i is then decided, with some verdict v.
  • Step 2: the second row is a schedule S′ with the same rounds and the same leader and kind at slot i, but other leaders elsewhere (blue). If slot j still commits the anchor and the slots between are still skipped under S′, slot i receives the same verdict v.
  • Each rule proves Indirect by cases on slot i's own candidate and the anchor's history, which a change of leaders elsewhere does not affect. From this stronger form, Descends is derived once (slide 62).

The support: the conditions for a commit

  • A support names the wave and what it means for a block to certify a candidate.
  • Law 1 (local): certification depends only on the wave's rounds, so it is unchanged by cutting or shifting rounds outside them.
  • Law 2 (commits): if a reliable quorum T leads a slot, produces through its wave and certifies every candidate, then a view that holds those rounds commits the slot.
  • Liveness uses this premise, live, at every slot T leads; the two network assumptions are used only to establish it.
  • A support describes what a rule's commit counts; two laws relate it to the rule's verdicts, and liveness is proved from them.
  • A support gives a wave length, the number of rounds between a candidate and its certifiers, and a relation stating when a block certifies a candidate. For Odontoceti, Nemo-Nemo and Hybrid the wave length is 1 and certification is a reference.
  • Law 1: across a cut or a shift that leaves the certifier's wave unchanged, a block certifies the same candidates as before.
  • Law 2, in the figure: T is a reliable quorum (bracket) that leads the slot of L (row v0), has a block at every round of the wave, and every T-block C at round r + wave certifies every candidate.
  • Step 1: a view that holds the rounds of the wave then commits the slot (badge commit L).
  • Step 2: the premise live requires this at every slot that T leads in a window, on a view that holds the window. Liveness uses only live.
  • Step 2: the lower diagram shows the two ways to establish live: from synchrony and coverage, by one theorem stated for every support, or from a reactive pacemaker execution, proved per rule. No synchrony assumption appears in the properties layer; a script checks this as part of the build.

Optional and derived properties

Optional, needed only by the mechanism named in parentheses:

  • CommitsDirect: a direct commit is a verdict (adaptive leader count).
  • SkipsUnsupported: a slot is skipped when a large enough set, present one round above, references none of its candidates (prompt skip).
  • Quorate: every block above genesis references blocks of n − f distinct creators (chain quality).
  • SelfParent, NoEquiv: every block references its creator's previous block; a reliable creator makes one block per round (inclusion).
  • Some properties are needed only by one mechanism; others are derived once from the core properties, so no rule has to prove them.
  • CommitsDirect: a candidate that passes the rule's direct-commit test is committed; adaptive leader count uses it. SkipsUnsupported: if a set of validators meeting the rule's size condition has blocks in the view one round above the slot and none of them references a candidate, the slot is skipped; prompt skip uses it.
  • Quorate: every non-genesis block references blocks of at least n − f distinct creators, under the standard fault model; chain quality uses it. SelfParent and NoEquiv: every non-genesis block references a block of its own creator, and a reliable creator has one block per round; inclusion uses them.

Safety, for every rule and every mechanism

From Banded, Agree and CommitsCandidate, across any sequence of cuts, recoveries and re-geneses:

  • every verdict above the settling round is the same before and after;
  • any view after agrees with any view before, above the settling round;
  • every commit is a candidate, and no block is committed at two slots;
  • adding blocks never changes a verdict.
  • The safety theorem: every rule with Banded, Agree and CommitsCandidate is safe across any composition of mechanisms that cut, fill or re-genesis the DAG. It is proved once, in Lean, for all eleven rules.
  • The settling round is the round from which the original and the transformed DAG hold the same blocks, up to a shift of round numbers; below it the mechanisms change the DAG.
  • In the diagram: the source DAG, the DAG after a fill (dashed blue: filled holes), and the DAG after a cut. The bottom row, verdicts of the slots above the settling round, is equal in all three.
  • The four conclusions: verdicts above the settling round are the same before and after, at the shifted slot numbers; any view after agrees with any view before above that round; every commit is a candidate, and no block is committed at two slots; and when blocks are added to the universe, a view and any larger view agree at every slot.
  • The ledger reading: two validators' decided prefixes above the settling round are equal. The next slide states liveness.

Liveness, for every rule

From Law 2, CommitsCandidate, SelfParent and NoEquiv:

  • Progress: every slot below a run of reliably led slots is decided, and reliably led slots continue to be committed.
  • Inclusion: every block of a reliable validator enters the ledger, through a later slot its creator leads.

Seven rules have both. Nemo-Nemo, FinWhale, Hydrozoan and Optimal-Hydrozoan lack SelfParent and have progress only.

  • The liveness theorem: from Law 2, CommitsCandidate, SelfParent and NoEquiv, a rule makes progress and includes every reliable validator's blocks, wherever the premise live holds.
  • Progress: if, past every point, the schedule has a run of consecutive slots led by members of a reliable quorum T, then every slot below such a run is decided, and slots led by T continue to be committed. Progress also assumes Descends, which is derived from Indirect.
  • Inclusion: under a schedule in which each reliable validator leads infinitely often, every block of a reliable validator enters the ledger.
  • Step 1: the diagram shows block b of a reliable validator v at round r; each later block of v references its predecessor (self-parent), up to the leader block of a later slot that v leads. When that slot is committed, b is in the leader block's history and enters the ledger. NoEquiv ensures that b is the only block of v at round r, so the chain passes through it.
  • Seven rules have both progress and inclusion. Nemo-Nemo, FinWhale, Hydrozoan and Optimal-Hydrozoan lack SelfParent and are proved to make progress only.
